*** 181. Introduction: What is Transfer Learning and Why Use It ***
## **Lecture Summary: Transfer Learning**

### **What is Transfer Learning?**

* Transfer learning is the process of taking a machine learning model trained on one task and adapting it to a different, but related, task.
* Instead of building models from scratch, we reuse the learned patterns (weights/parameters) from an existing model trained on a large dataset.
* This is especially useful when you don’t have a massive dataset for your specific problem.

---

### **Example Use Case: Computer Vision**

* **Task**: Classify food images (e.g., Food Vision Mini).
* **Source Model**: A model trained on the **ImageNet** dataset (over 1 million images, 1,000 classes).
* **Approach**:

  * Use the patterns learned from ImageNet.
  * Customize (fine-tune) those patterns for the food classification task.
* **Benefit**: The model already knows general visual features (edges, textures, object shapes), which can be fine-tuned for more specific use cases.

---

### **Example Use Case: Natural Language Processing**

* **Task**: Build a spam detection model.
* **Source Model**: A language model trained on **Wikipedia**.
* **Approach**:

  * Use the pre-trained model’s understanding of language structure (syntax, semantics).
  * Customize it for email classification (e.g., spam vs. not spam).
* **Example**: In a sentence like *“The \_\_\_ jumped over the fence”*, the model might predict “dog” as a likely word due to learned context.

---

### **Key Concept**

* **Transfer Learning** = Pre-train a model on a large, general dataset → Fine-tune on a smaller, task-specific dataset.

---

### **Why Use Transfer Learning?**

#### **Main Benefits**

1. **Leverage Proven Architectures**

   * Use models that have been carefully designed and tested (e.g., EfficientNet, ResNet, Vision Transformers).
2. **Reuse Learned Patterns**

   * Instead of learning from scratch, start with pre-learned features that are often generalizable.
3. **Save Resources**

   * Requires **less data**, **less compute**, and **less time** to get good performance.
4. **Achieve Better Results**

   * Often leads to better model performance compared to training from scratch.

---

### **Research Support**

#### **From “How to Train Your ViT” (Vision Transformer)**

* Most practitioners **use publicly available pre-trained models** rather than training from scratch.
* **Key Quote**:

  > “Transfer learning remains the best available option, even if the downstream data is only weakly related to the pretraining data.”

---

*** 186. Turning Our Data into DataLoaders with Manually Created Transforms ***
## Lecture Notes: Creating Data Loaders and Manual Transforms for Pretrained Models

### **1. Context Recap**

* In the last session, we:

  * Downloaded the **Pizza, Steak, and Sushi** dataset.
  * Created directory paths for **train** and **test** sets.

---

### **2. Objective**

* Convert image data into **PyTorch data loaders** using a function called `create_dataloaders()` from `data_setup.py` in the `going_modular` directory.

---

### **3. Dataset Loader Setup**

* The `create_dataloaders()` function:

  * Takes in `train_dir`, `test_dir`, and a **transform**.
  * Uses `ImageFolder` to wrap datasets.
  * Returns: `train_dataloader`, `test_dataloader`, and `class_names`.

---

### **4. Image Transformations**

#### **Why Transforms Matter**

* **Important Principle**: When using a **pre-trained model**, your input data must be transformed in **the same way** the model’s training data was.
* Otherwise, performance will degrade due to distribution mismatch.

---

### **5. Two Ways to Define Transforms**

> Requires **torchvision ≥ 0.13**

#### 1. **Manual Transform Definition**

* You define the preprocessing pipeline.
* Advantage: **Flexible** — you can tailor it to your model or experiment.
* Disadvantage: **Error-prone** — mismatched transforms can hurt performance.

#### 2. **Automatic Transform from Model Weights**

* Transform is predefined based on the model's weights (discussed in next lecture).

---

### **6. Manual Transform Details**

#### **Transform Requirements**

For most torchvision pre-trained models:

* Images should be:

  * RGB (3 channels)
  * Resized to at least `224x224`
  * Pixel values scaled from 0–1
  * Normalized using ImageNet statistics:

    * **Mean**: `[0.485, 0.456, 0.406]`
    * **Std**: `[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]`

#### **Code Example**

-------------------code----------------------
from torchvision import transforms

manual_transforms = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.Resize((224, 224)),  # Resize images
    transforms.ToTensor(),          # Convert to tensor and scale to [0, 1]
    transforms.Normalize(
        mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], 
        std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]
    )  # Match ImageNet distribution
])
-------------------code----------------------

#### **Usage with Data Loader Function**

-------------------code----------------------
from going_modular.going_modular import data_setup

train_dataloader, test_dataloader, class_names = data_setup.create_dataloaders(
    train_dir=train_dir,
    test_dir=test_dir,
    transform=manual_transforms
)
-------------------code----------------------

---

### **7. Why Use These Specific Values?**

* These **mean** and **standard deviation** values come from the **ImageNet dataset**.
* ImageNet is a massive image database used to train many pretrained models.
* Normalizing with these values ensures our data distribution matches what the model expects.

*** 187. Turning Our Data into DataLoaders with Automatic Created Transforms ***
## Lecture Summary: Preparing Data for Pre-trained Models (Torchvision v0.13+)

### Key Concepts

#### 1. **Importance of Matching Pre-trained Model Data Format**

* Pre-trained models expect input data in the same format as their training data.
* Mismatched preprocessing can lead to **performance degradation**.

---

### Manual Transforms

* You can manually create a transform pipeline using `transforms.Compose([...])`.
* Manual transforms offer **flexibility**: you can add any preprocessing, data augmentation, etc.
* **Risk**: If the transform doesn’t match the model’s expected input format, it may reduce performance.

---

### Automatic Transforms (Torchvision 0.13+)

Introduced in `torchvision v0.13`, this new feature lets you automatically create the correct transforms from the pre-trained model's weights.

#### Benefits:

* Ensures **consistency** with the model’s training.
* **Reduces errors** due to manual misconfiguration.
* Easily switch between models without rewriting preprocessing logic.

#### Usage:

1. **Install/check your torchvision version:**

   -------------------code----------------------
   import torchvision
   print(torchvision.__version__)  # Should be >= 0.13
   -------------------code----------------------

2. **Import model and weights:**

   -------------------code----------------------
   from torchvision.models import efficientnet_b0, EfficientNet_B0_Weights
   weights = EfficientNet_B0_Weights.DEFAULT  # Defaults to best available weights
   -------------------code----------------------

3. **Get automatic transforms:**

   -------------------code----------------------
   auto_transforms = weights.transforms()
   -------------------code----------------------

4. **Create data loaders with automatic transforms:**

   -------------------code----------------------
   from torchvision import datasets
   from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

   train_dataset = datasets.ImageFolder(root=train_dir, transform=auto_transforms)
   test_dataset = datasets.ImageFolder(root=test_dir, transform=auto_transforms)

   train_loader = DataLoader(train_dataset, batch_size=32, shuffle=True)
   test_loader = DataLoader(test_dataset, batch_size=32)
   -------------------code----------------------

---

### Why This Matters

* This approach simplifies the workflow and ensures **high accuracy** with the appropriate normalization, resizing, cropping, etc.
* Using `EfficientNet_B0_Weights.DEFAULT` leverages the version trained on **ImageNet-1K** (1000-class ImageNet dataset).

  * Top-1 Accuracy: \~77.6%
  * Top-5 Accuracy: \~93%

---

### EfficientNet Overview

* Model series: `B0` to `B7`
* **Bigger number = Larger model**
* **Tradeoff**:

  * Higher accuracy → Larger size → Slower inference/deployment
  * Start small (e.g., B0) and scale up **only if necessary**

  > *Experiment, experiment, experiment.*

#### **Core Principle of Transfer Learning:**

> Use a well-performing model trained on a similar task, and fine-tune it for your problem.

### **4. Key Factors When Choosing a Model**

1. **Speed** – How fast does it run? Important for real-time applications.
2. **Size** – Number of parameters; affects deployability and hardware requirements.
3. **Performance** – How well does it solve your task?

These factors are **interrelated**. The ideal model balances:

> **Speed**, **Size**, and **Performance**

---

### **5. Deployment Considerations**

* **Where will the model run?**

  * **On-device** (e.g., mobile phones, self-driving cars): Must be small and fast.

---

### **6. Evaluating Model Trade-offs**

* When comparing models:

  * Look at **Accuracy** (performance)
  * Look at **Number of Parameters** (size)
  * Use **accuracy-to-parameter ratio** to evaluate efficiency

#### Example Comparison:

* **EfficientNet B0**:

  * \~78% accuracy
  * \~5 million parameters
* **Inception V3**:

  * Lower accuracy
  * \~6x more parameters than EfficientNet B0

> **EfficientNet B0** offers high performance with fewer parameters – a strong candidate.

---

### **7. Alternative Models**

---

### **9. General vs Specific Models**

* In constrained environments: prefer **efficient, compact models**
* With **infinite compute**: opt for the **largest, most general** model possible

#### The “Bitter Lesson” (Richard Sutton):

> General methods that leverage more computation and data ultimately outperform human-designed heuristics.

---

### **10. Summary – What to Keep in Mind**

When selecting a model, always evaluate:

1. **Speed** – Required inference time
2. **Size** – Deployment hardware and memory constraints
3. **Performance** – Accuracy or other relevant metrics

*** 189. Setting Up a Pre-trained Model with Torch-vision ***
## **Lecture 3.2: Setting Up a Pre-trained Model (EfficientNet B0)**

### **1. Choosing a Pre-trained Model**

* Selecting the right pre-trained model can be challenging.
* The course uses **EfficientNet B0** as a default model.
* Most pre-trained models are derived from research papers and integrated into PyTorch if proven effective.
* Models in the **TorchVision library** are generally well-tested and reliable.

---

### **2. Instantiating EfficientNet B0**

#### **Old Method (Before TorchVision v0.13)**

-------------------code----------------------
import torchvision.models as models

model = models.efficientnet_b0(pretrained=True)
-------------------code----------------------

* This method is deprecated.
* `pretrained=True` will raise a warning and be removed in v0.15.

#### **New Method (TorchVision v0.13 and Above)**

-------------------code----------------------
from torchvision.models import efficientnet_b0, EfficientNet_B0_Weights

weights = EfficientNet_B0_Weights.DEFAULT
model = efficientnet_b0(weights=weights)
-------------------code----------------------

* Uses `weights=...` instead of `pretrained=True`.
* `DEFAULT` selects the best available pretrained weights.
* Different versions of weights (e.g., `IMAGENET1K_V1`, `V2`) are available and can be selected as needed.

---

### **3. Model Architecture Overview**

#### **EfficientNet B0 Model Structure**

* The model has many layers:

  * **Convolutional Layers**
  * **Activation Layers (SiLU)**
  * **BatchNorm Layers**
* These are all pre-defined and built into the architecture via transfer learning.

#### **Sections of the Model**

-------------------code----------------------
model.features       # Feature extractor layers
model.avgpool        # Global average pooling layer
model.classifier     # Fully connected classification head
-------------------code----------------------

* **`features`**: Extracts patterns and features from images (e.g., edges, textures).
* **`avgpool`**: Compresses extracted features into a single 1280-length vector.
* **`classifier`**: Converts the 1280-length vector into prediction logits.

---

### What’s Inside the Model?**

Think of the model as made of 3 parts:

1. **Features**

   * Looks at the image and picks up useful patterns.
   * Like eyes recognizing shapes and textures.

2. **Average Pool**

   * Compresses all the features into one smaller vector.
   * Like summarizing everything the model saw into a few important numbers.

3. **Classifier**

   * Makes the final decision: “Is this a pizza, steak, or sushi?”
   * Originally, this part predicts 1,000 types (ImageNet classes).
   * We change it to only predict **3** types (our classes).

### **4. Customizing the Model**

#### **ImageNet Limitation**

* The default classifier is built for **ImageNet**, which has **1,000 classes**:

-------------------code----------------------
model.classifier = nn.Linear(in_features=1280, out_features=1000)
-------------------code----------------------

#### **Adapting to Custom Dataset**

* For a custom dataset with 3 classes (e.g., **pizza, steak, sushi**), the classifier should be modified:

-------------------code----------------------
model.classifier = nn.Linear(in_features=1280, out_features=3)
-------------------code----------------------

---

What Do We Change?**

* We **keep** the first two parts (features and average pool).
* We **replace** the last part (classifier) to match our problem.

This means:

* Input is 1280 numbers (from the image).
* Output is 3 numbers (our 3 classes).



### **5. Concept of Feature Extraction**

* A **Feature Extractor** (also called a **Backbone**) is a truncated version of a full model:

  * Keeps all layers in `features`.
  * Optionally replaces or fine-tunes the classifier layer.

  in other words:
  * A **feature extractor** is a model that we use to “see” the image and pull out useful parts.
  * We don’t change most of the model—just the last layer.
  * It’s like using a brain that already knows how to see things, and only teaching it new labels.


#### **Understanding Features**

* Features can be patterns like:

  * Circular shape for pizza.
  * Darker texture for steak.
  * Small rectangular shapes for sushi.

#### **Transformation Pipeline**

1. **Image → Feature Extractor (features)**: Extract raw patterns.
2. **→ Average Pooling (avgpool)**: Compress patterns into a single vector.
3. **→ Classifier**: Map to class probabilities.

### **6. Summary of Transfer Learning in this Setup**

* EfficientNet B0 is used as a **feature extractor**.
* All pre-trained layers (except classifier) are retained.
* Only the **final classifier layer is modified** to suit the custom dataset.
* This method is efficient and leverages the power of deep, pretrained architectures with minimal training.

## **2. Key Concepts in Transfer Learning**

### **A. Terminology**

* **Backbone**: Core architecture of a model (e.g., EfficientNet B0).
* **Feature Extractor**: A model used to extract learned features from input data.
* **Feature**: Patterns learned from data (e.g., the curve of a pizza slice, color of steak).
* **Frozen Layers**: Layers whose parameters are not updated during training.

---

### **B. Transfer Learning Types**

| Type               | Description                                                                 |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Original Model     | Pretrained model (e.g., EfficientNet on ImageNet).                          |
| Feature Extraction | Use base model with frozen layers; only the output layer is trained.        |
| Fine Tuning        | Unfreeze and train some of the base layers in addition to the output layer. |

---

## **3. EfficientNet B0 Architecture Overview**

### **Structure**

* **Features Section**: Extracts patterns from images.
* **Average Pooling Layer**: Converts the feature tensor to a single feature vector.

  * For EfficientNet B0, this vector has size **1280**.
* **Classifier Head**: Final layer mapping the feature vector to output classes.

  * Pretrained output: 1000 classes (ImageNet).
  * Custom output: Can be changed (e.g., 3 classes for pizza, steak, sushi).

---

## **4. Feature Extraction Process**

### **A. Model Preparation**

* Use pretrained EfficientNet B0 from `torchvision.models`.
* Freeze **all base layers** (feature extraction layers).
* Replace **classifier head** with a new one suited to the target task.

### **B. Example**

* Input: Custom dataset (e.g., pizza, steak, sushi).
* Feature extraction layers remain frozen.
* Classifier head is updated to match new classes (e.g., 3 output neurons).

---

## **5. Fine Tuning Overview**

* Suitable for **larger datasets**.
* Involves unfreezing some of the base layers and retraining them.
* Typically done **gradually**, layer by layer from top to bottom.

---

## **6. Summary of Transfer Learning Workflow**

1. Start with a **pretrained model** (original/foundation model).
2. Apply **feature extraction**:

   * Freeze feature layers.
   * Replace and train the output layer with new data.
3. Optionally apply **fine tuning**:

   * Unfreeze and train some feature layers if more data is available.

## **Feature Extraction with EfficientNet-B0**

### **1. Model Summary Overview**

* Each layer contains parameters that help extract useful features from input images.

### **2. Purpose of Feature Extraction**

* In feature extraction, we **freeze the base layers** of a pre-trained model.
* These layers retain learned patterns (from ImageNet, in this case).
* Only the **classifier head** is updated to match our specific classification task (e.g., pizza, steak, sushi).

---

### **3. Freezing the Base Layers**

* To freeze layers:

  -------------------code----------------------
  for param in model.features.parameters():
      param.requires_grad = False
  -------------------code----------------------
* `requires_grad = False` disables gradient tracking so the optimizer won’t update these weights during training.
* This preserves the pre-trained knowledge from ImageNet.
* We verified this by checking that only the output layer remains trainable after freezing.

---

### **4. Updating the Classifier Head**

* We need to **customize the output layer** to match our dataset (with 3 classes).
* Input size to the classifier is 1280 (from EfficientNet), so we must match that in the new `Linear` layer.
* Example updated classifier:

  -------------------code----------------------
  model.classifier = nn.Sequential(
      nn.Dropout(p=0.2, inplace=True),
      nn.Linear(in_features=1280, out_features=len(class_names))  # e.g., 3 classes
  )
  -------------------code----------------------
* Set a random seed before defining layers to ensure **reproducibility**:

  -------------------code----------------------
  torch.manual_seed(42)
  -------------------code----------------------
* The model is moved to the correct device (e.g., GPU) for training:

  -------------------code----------------------
  model = model.to(device)
  -------------------code----------------------

### **5. Explanation of Dropout**

### What is Dropout?
**Dropout** is a regularization technique used in deep learning to prevent **overfitting**.

### **In simple terms:**

During training, **dropout randomly turns off (sets to zero) some neurons** in a layer. This means those neurons don't participate in that round of learning.

### **Why is this useful?**

If the model relies too much on specific neurons, it may memorize the training data instead of learning general patterns — this is **overfitting**.

By randomly "dropping out" neurons:

* The model is **forced to learn in a more distributed way**.
* It becomes **more robust**.
* It learns to **not rely on any single neuron** too much.

### **Analogy:**

Think of dropout like practicing a team sport where random players sit out each practice session. The team learns to play well **regardless of who’s missing**, which makes them stronger overall.

### **Key points:**

* Dropout is **only active during training**, not during testing or inference.
* You can control how much is dropped out using a **dropout rate** (e.g., 0.5 means half the neurons are randomly turned off).



* **Dropout** is a **regularization** technique used during training to prevent **overfitting**.
* It randomly zeros out neurons with a probability `p` during each forward pass.
* This prevents the network from becoming too reliant on specific neurons and encourages learning more robust patterns.

**Key Quote from the Lecture:**

> "Dropout removes some neurons at random, so the remaining ones hopefully learn more generalizable patterns."
